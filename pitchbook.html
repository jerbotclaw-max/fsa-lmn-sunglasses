1 · The clinical case for UV attenuation as cataract prevention

  • Mechanism: UVR (esp. UV-B) reaches the lens and drives photo-oxidative damage — protein cross-linking, yellowing, aggregation → cortical and posterior subcapsular opacities.UV-damage lens review: PMC 2024.
  • Epidemiology: Dose-dependent association of ambient UVR with cataract, strongest for cortical cataract. Delcourt et al., Alienor cohort (IOVS 2014): lifetime ambient UV → cataract extraction risk. McCarty & Taylor 2000 (Br J Ophthalmol 84:735-40) for ocular surface.
  • Attributable burden: WHO/ILO analyses estimate up to ~20% of annual cataract blindness sun-related; occupational solar UVR systematic reviews put cortical-cataract attributable fraction as high as 25% (Lucas 2006 basis; PMC 6586024).
  • Intervention: UV400 eyewear blocks essentially all UVR at the cornea/lens. Taylor 1988 (NEJM 319:1429) established the exposure→cataract dose-response; protective eyewear is the directly implied intervention. Primary-prevention RCTs don't exist (decades-long follow-up, unethical exposure arms), so the evidence is observational — strong but not trial-grade. That's the honest ceiling of the claim.
Where prevention stops and the FSA line begins: Pub 502 pays for treating medical conditions, not preventing hypothetical ones. "UV400 to prevent cataracts" is a marketing truth but a losing LMN. The clinical-to-benefits bridge is found pathology + symptoms: early lens changes with glare (variant E/L), photophobia (A/F), post-op protection (B). The pitch book below therefore sells two different products to two different audiences — prevention to wellness buyers, treatment documentation to the FSA vertical.

2 · The Truemed playbook, decomposed

  1. Questionnaire → practitioner review → LMN. 2-minute intake, licensed practitioner reviews, letter if the condition is plausibly documented ($25-35). Key design: the evaluation is real, scoped, and cheap — volume with low marginal cost per letter.
  2. Checkout integration. Partner brands embed Truemed at payment ("pay with HSA/FSA"), so the letter arrives before the claim; conversion jumps because the friction moves from post-hoc reimbursement to point-of-sale.
  3. Administrator-agnostic letters. Letters are written to generic Pub-502 criteria so they survive any TPA's review.
  4. Category expansion. One compliance engine, many verticals (fitness, nutrition, sleep, recovery). The LMN is the reusable asset.

3 · Expanded playbook — UV/tinted eyewear vertical

Phase 1 — Clinical assets (built: this site)

12 LMN templates spanning 11 diagnoses; provider kit with state-telehealth checklist; denial-letter template; citations.

Phase 2 — Provider network

Recruit ODs (optometrists are the right density/price point) into a flat-fee telehealth evaluation pool. IMLC multi-state licenses first (covers ~40+ states). Payment per evaluation, never per letter (B&P § 650; AKS-shaped risk). Target fee: $25-30.

Phase 3 — Point-of-sale integration

Embed the intake at optical checkout (Zenni/Warby-style DTC, sunglasses brands, optometry chains). Customer pays with FSA/HSA card at purchase; LMN arrives within 24-48h; receipt+LMN submitted. Kill-switch: intake answers that fail the clinical screen route to "this letter isn't appropriate for you" — the legal firewall from the provider kit.

Phase 4 — Employer & wellness channel (prevention product)

Cataract/UV prevention is a legitimate wellness-benefit pitch: outdoor-workforce employers (construction, agriculture, delivery) fund UV400 eyewear as PPE/wellness — no FSA needed, no LMN needed, OSHA eye-protection framing. This is where the pure prevention science lives, cleanly separated from the tax-advantaged channel.

Phase 5 — Data moat

Outcome tracking: symptom scores pre/post (migraine days, glare scores), claim approval rates by administrator, variant-level acceptance. The approval-rate dataset becomes the sales asset no template farm has.
Failure modes (from the enforcement record): per-letter payments; questionnaires that can't produce a "no"; marketing "guaranteed approval" (FTC § 5); unlicensed interstate issuance (Hageseth); letters without chart retention; expanding from genuine evaluation to rubber-stamping as volume pressure rises. The 2020 telehealth takedowns all followed that arc. The moat is the discipline, not the template.
