Provider Kit — Compliant Telehealth LMN Issuance

How a licensed provider can offer low-cost/free telehealth evaluations for UV-filter & tinted-eyewear LMNs — the defensible way, with the laws and precedent that define the line · Aug 29, 2026
What this kit is not. It is not a "sign for anyone" pipeline. A letter certifying a condition the provider did not reasonably evaluate — or that the patient does not have — is not a loophole; it is potential health-care fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1347), mail/wire fraud exposure for the claim submission (§ 1341, § 1343), and for the patient, a false statement on a benefits claim with tax consequences (FSAs/HSAs are tax-advantaged under IRC §§ 105/106/125/223 (HSA); see also § 4976 (nondiscrimination excise) and § 4980D, plus state false-claims acts (e.g., CA IFPA, NY FCA) that reach private-plan claims; ineligible reimbursements are taxable distributions and misstatements to the plan administrator can constitute plan fraud). The DOJ has repeatedly prosecuted telehealth executives and prescribers for exactly this pattern — see the June 2020 National Health Care Fraud Takedown, where telemedicine-marketing and prescribing schemes drew 186 defendants and alleged losses exceeding $4.5 billion (justice.gov). "Easy" was the product. The indictments were the receipt.
What is genuinely legal and easy: a licensed provider conducting a real evaluation — which can be asynchronous — limited to a narrow clinical question ("does this patient have photophobia or a UV-sensitive condition that tinted UV400/FL-41 eyewear would treat?"), documenting it, and issuing the letter when clinically true. Photophobia is still a diagnosis that must be documented: require a corroborating record (migraine or ocular diagnosis, exam or consult note) — history alone is exactly the dual-use comfort claim that gets denied and investigated. The two halves of this page explain both.

Part 1 — The compliant workflow (provider-facing)

  1. Licensure first. The provider must be licensed in the patient's state. Interstate practice is governed by state law; the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC, ~40+ member states) expedited multi-state licensure, and similar compacts exist for nurses (NLC) — but "licensed in the patient's state" is the baseline. An out-of-state physician issuing an LMN for a California patient without CA licensure or telehealth registration invites discipline. (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 2367 — telehealth satisfaction of the exam requirement; § 2290.5 definitions; CMA guidance.)
  2. Establish the relationship & standard of care. Most states now permit a provider-patient relationship via telehealth, including asynchronous store-and-forward, for non-controlled, low-risk evaluations — if the encounter meets the same standard of care as in-person. California's Telehealth Act (Bus. & Prof. Code § 2290.5) and the federal Telehealth Modernization direction post-2020 support this; the standard of care does not drop because the medium changed.
  3. Structured, honest intake. Use a condition-specific questionnaire: symptom frequency/triggers (bright light → pain, headache, squinting), migraine history, surgical history (cataract, refractive), ocular diagnoses, occupational exposure. Ask questions whose answers you can defend. Do not design intake so that "no" answers still produce a letter — that questionnaire is Exhibit A in any enforcement action.
  4. Examine or refer out. For LMNs tied to exam findings (post-cataract, retinal disease, early lens changes — templates B/C/E), require records from an examining provider (patient-supplied chart notes/vision reports) or refer to a participating optometrist network. Asynchronous review of existing records is itself recognized telehealth practice.
  5. Certify only what you verified. Sign the LMN (template A–E) with your NPI, keep it in the chart, retain records to your state's retention standard (CA: ≥7 years for adults, B&P Code § 2020(z) via CMA guidance).
  6. Fee structure. A modest flat fee ($25–35) for a genuine evaluation is standard (Truemed's published model). "Free" to the patient is fine when the service is genuinely charitable or subsidized — but a provider paid per-issued-letter by an eyewear seller has a Stark/anti-kickback-shaped problem if federal program business is in the loop, and a state anti-kickback/fee-splitting problem regardless (B&P Code § 650). Structure payment for the evaluation, never per letter.

Part 2 — The legal line, with citations

AuthorityWhat it means here
IRS Pub. 502 — medical expensesEyeglasses "needed for medical reasons" qualify; the statute/regulations never name sunglasses. Non-Rx tinted eyewear sits in administrator-discretion territory, which is why the LMN exists — and why the administrator can still deny.
IRC §§ 105, 106, 125 & regsFSA/HSA are employer-sponsored tax-exempt vehicles. Ineligible reimbursements are includible in gross income; the plan administrator bears audit exposure, which is exactly why administrators scrutinize dual-use items.

United States v. three telehealth executives in the 2020 Takedown (M.D. Fla.) is the template: marketing funnels + light-touch prescriber review = charged scheme.

Superior Court (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 1339Leading internet-prescribing precedent: an out-of-state physician who prescribed via an online questionnaire without examination was held subject to California discipline and civil liability — the standard of care applies to web-based practice. The foundational "online questionnaire is not a shield" case.

Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 2290.5, 2367; § 650 (fee-splitting/anti-kickback)Telehealth establishes the practitioner-patient relationship when standards are met; telehealth satisfies the exam requirement where appropriate; per-letter payments to a referrer split professional fees — prohibited regardless of federal-program nexus.
State telehealth modalities rules (e.g., Fed. CCHP compendium)Most states expressly permit asynchronous evaluation for non-controlled care; a handful require synchronous audio-video for a new relationship. The kit's checklist step 0 is: look up the patient's state in the CCHP compendium.
FTC Act § 5If a service markets "guaranteed LMN approval," that's a deceptive claim — administrators have discretion and approvals are not guaranteed. Market accuracy, not certainty.
